Small business website in India: what to budget for and what to skip

A practical guide to budgeting for a small business website in India: what to pay for first, what to skip and the hidden costs to ask about.

If you run a small business in India, you have probably heard a wide range of numbers for a website. Some are surprisingly low and some are shockingly high. This guide will not give you a price, because the right price depends on what your website needs to do. Instead it helps you decide what to pay for first, what to leave for later, and which costs people forget until the invoice arrives.

Start with the job, not the price

A website is a tool, and a tool should be bought for a job. Before you ask anyone for a quote, finish this sentence: "In the first year, my website should help me ___." Common answers for a small business are:

  • Let new customers find us on Google.
  • Show our work or products so people trust us before they call.
  • Make it easy to enquire on WhatsApp.
  • Take orders or bookings without a phone call.

Each of these jobs costs a different amount. A website that shows your work and takes enquiries is a smaller project than one that takes payments or manages bookings. Starting from the job stops you buying features you will never use.

What to pay for first

A clear, credible design. Visitors judge a business within seconds. A clean layout, your own photographs and plain language do more than animations or effects.

A fast, mobile-friendly build. Most of your visitors will arrive on a phone. A site that loads slowly or breaks on small screens loses people before they read a word.

An easy way to contact you. A WhatsApp button, a phone link and a short form. This is the part that turns visits into business.

A structure Google can read. Titles, headings and page structure that search engines can understand cost little when built in from the start and are painful to add later.

Ownership. You should own the website files, the design files and the logins. Make this a condition of hiring.

What to skip, or leave for later

  • A blog, until you have something to say. An empty blog looks worse than none.
  • Online payments, unless customers want to pay online. Many service businesses and wholesalers sell on a conversation. Payments can be added later.
  • Multiple languages, until you have buyers who need them.
  • Heavy animation and video backgrounds. They slow the site and rarely persuade.
  • Features copied from big companies. Your website should fit your business, not theirs.

The hidden costs to ask about

Many small business owners are surprised not by the quote but by the costs around it. Ask about each of these before you sign.

  1. Domain and hosting. Who pays, who owns the account, and what is the renewal cost?
  2. Content. Who writes the text and who takes or edits the photographs?
  3. Changes after launch. Is there a period of support included, and what does a change cost afterwards?
  4. Security and updates. Who keeps the site secure and up to date?
  5. Payment gateway fees. If you take payments, the gateway charges its own fees separate from the developer's.
  6. Ending the relationship. Can you take your website elsewhere if you want to?

How to get comparable quotes

Give every developer the same short brief: what you sell, who buys, how buyers reach you today, what the site should do, and any websites you like. Ask each to list what is included and what is not. If one quote is far lower than the others, find out what is missing rather than assuming you found a bargain.

Frequently asked questions

Can I build my own website to save money?

You can, using a website builder, and for a very simple need it may be enough. The trade-off is time, limited design control and the risk of a site that looks like everyone else's.

How many pages does a small business website need?

Often five to eight: Home, About, Services or Products, Work or Gallery, Contact, and any legal pages you require. Add pages when you have something genuinely useful to put on them.

Should I pay monthly or once?

Both models exist. A one-time build with optional monthly care is common. Whichever you choose, make sure the proposal says what is included and who owns the result.
