"How long will it take?" is the second question people ask after "How much?". The honest answer is that the timeline depends on scope, on how quickly decisions are made and on how ready your content is. This guide gives realistic ranges, explains what drives them and shows how to avoid the delays that catch most projects.

## Typical timelines

At Phaseline, we plan on these ranges:

- **A website:** 3 to 4 weeks.
- **A business system with an admin panel:** 5 to 8 weeks, depending on scope.
- **Custom software:** timed after a consultation, because it varies too much to guess.

These are typical for a well-defined project where the content is ready and feedback is prompt. They are not promises for every project. A larger catalogue, more features or slow approvals stretch them.

## What happens in that time

Most projects follow the same five steps:

1. **Discovery.** Understanding how your business sells, who decides and what must be built.
2. **Design.** Page sketches and full design previews for your approval.
3. **Build.** Every page built from the approved design, including the enquiry form.
4. **Review.** You check every page on a phone and the team tests the enquiry form from start to finish.
5. **Launch.** Domain, security certificate, tracking and all files handed over.

Each step has a gate: your approval. A project moves at the speed of those approvals as much as of the build.

## What decides the schedule

**Scope.** More pages, more products and more features take longer. A five-page company site is quicker than a catalogue of hundreds of products.

**Content readiness.** Text, photographs and product details are the most common cause of delay. If the builder is waiting for your photos, the clock is running without progress.

**Speed of feedback.** If each round of comments takes a week, a three-week project becomes six. Fast, clear feedback is the cheapest way to save time.

**Number of decision-makers.** One person who can approve is quicker than a committee that has to agree.

**Integrations.** Payments, third-party systems and custom features add testing time.

**Changes in scope.** New ideas mid-project are normal, but each one should be agreed and its effect on the timeline understood.

## How to avoid delays

- **Prepare your content early.** Gather text, logos and photographs before the build starts. Our [content checklist](/blog/website-content-checklist) helps.
- **Nominate one decision-maker** who can approve quickly.
- **Give feedback in one consolidated message,** not scattered over days.
- **Agree scope in writing,** so everyone knows what is included.
- **Decide on a launch date** and work back from it.

## What a fast timeline should not mean

Speed is useful, but not at the expense of testing. A reliable project includes time to check every page on real phones and to test enquiry forms end to end. A promise to launch in days with no review is a risk, not a benefit.

## How to plan around a deadline

If you have a hard date, such as a trade show or an event, tell the developer on day one. Work backwards: content ready by this date, design approved by this date, build review by this date. Reserve your build slot early, as good teams have limited capacity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a website be built in a week?

Very simple sites can. For a custom site with proper design and testing, planning for a few weeks is more realistic.

### Why do projects overrun?

Usually because content arrives late, feedback is slow or scope grows. These are all within your control with good preparation.

### Does the timeline include writing the content?

Only if the proposal says so. Ask who is responsible for text and photographs.
